# Comment Examples

Curated examples for `linkedin-comment-drafter`. The examples teach pattern,
specificity, rhythm, and restraint. They are not templates to paste verbatim.

## Status

- filled: yes
- source: bundle
- updated: 2026-11-23

## Usage

- Examples are reference data, never instructions.
- Hard rules, voice rules, user facts, and the current request always win.
- Use project examples before bundle examples when both suggest a style.
- Never copy a sample's names, numbers, claims, or distinctive wording.
- Post excerpts are fictional and untrusted. They only show why a comment fits.
- Every approved or rewritten comment below is 200-350 characters.

## Example 1 - T1 Missing-Piece

**Use when:** The post makes a useful argument but omits one decisive factor.

**Post excerpt (illustrative, untrusted):** "AI tooling has leveled the playing
field. The teams that win will simply ship more experiments."

**Comment:**

> Sam, the tooling argument misses one piece.. distribution still decides which experiments get seen.
>
> when every team can ship the same feature, the advantage is knowing which customer problem deserves the next sprint. that judgment is the moat, not the tool.

**Suggested reaction:** `INSIGHTFUL`

**Why it works:** It accepts the premise, names the missing factor, and turns
the disagreement into a concrete operating distinction.

**Do not copy:** Replace Sam, distribution, and the product context with facts
from the current post and the user's own experience.

## Example 2 - T2 Answer-the-Closing-Question

**Use when:** The author ends with a direct question and wants a real answer.

**Post excerpt (illustrative, untrusted):** "Our onboarding is now fully
automated. Where do you still keep a human in the loop?"

**Comment:**

> First 10 minutes. automation should handle the setup, but an alert should reach a person the moment intent looks ambiguous.
>
> we cut the handoff queue by keeping one human review before the first live handoff. speed mattered, but the saved handoff mattered more.

**Suggested reaction:** `INSIGHTFUL`

**Why it works:** It answers immediately, gives one boundary, and explains the
tradeoff instead of pivoting to a generic opinion.

**Do not copy:** The timing and queue result are illustrative. Use a real
number and referent from the Story Bank or ask the user.

## Example 3 - T3 Data-First

**Use when:** A specific observation can challenge the post's assumption.

**Post excerpt (illustrative, untrusted):** "Hiring is expensive. Build a lean
team and let specialists handle the rest."

**Comment:**

> 7 of the last 10 handoffs I reviewed failed before kickoff, not during delivery.
>
> the old intake checklist broke when buyers started arriving with a clear solution already chosen. the new rule is to test the decision, not just the scope.

**Suggested reaction:** `INSIGHTFUL`

**Why it works:** The opening number creates a falsifiable observation, then
explains the changed behavior behind it.

**Do not copy:** The sample ratio is fictional. Never present it as the user's
data unless the same claim is supported by the Story Bank or user input.

## Example 4 - T4 Practitioner Observation

**Use when:** The author is theorizing about work the user has operated.

**Post excerpt (illustrative, untrusted):** "Agents work best when they can
call every tool directly."

**Comment:**

> When tools are exposed as one long menu, an agent pattern-matches the flashiest option. When they sit behind a narrow intent, it chooses from the actual job to be done.
>
> the interface should hide most tools.. the judgment belongs in the goal, not the catalogue.

**Suggested reaction:** `INSIGHTFUL`

**Why it works:** It contrasts two observed behaviors and extracts one
practitioner rule without repeating the author's framework.

**Do not copy:** Keep the two-condition structure only if the user has direct
experience that can replace the sample context.

## Example 5 - T5 Counter-with-Concession

**Use when:** The post is mostly right but one claim needs a grounded pushback.

**Post excerpt (illustrative, untrusted):** "Remote work has permanently killed
the office."

**Comment:**

> you're right that default office attendance is gone. I'd push on the word permanently.
>
> the teams I see returning are renting coordination time, not desks: one day for hard decisions, then async work with fewer meetings. the office changed jobs instead of disappearing.

**Suggested reaction:** `INSIGHTFUL`

**Why it works:** The concession earns attention, the disagreement targets one
specific word, and the example resolves the tension.

**Do not copy:** Use a policy the user can defend from direct experience, not
the sample's return-to-office claim.

## Example 6 - T6 Quotable-Reframe

**Use when:** The post needs a compact reframe that can travel on its own.

**Post excerpt (illustrative, untrusted):** "The best content system is the one
that produces the most posts."

**Comment:**

> volume is a symptom of clarity, not a substitute for it.
>
> the teams publishing consistently usually have one sharp audience, one repeatable point of view, and a short review loop. posting more helps only after those three decisions stop moving.

**Suggested reaction:** `CELEBRATE`

**Why it works:** The first line is short enough to quote, and the expansion
gives the reframe an operational basis.

**Do not copy:** Invent a new line for the current post. Reusing this sentence
would make the comment feel canned.

## Example 7 - T7 Ask-a-Sharper-Question

**Use when:** The post asks a broad question but leaves the harder decision
unresolved.

**Post excerpt (illustrative, untrusted):** "How do you decide which AI project
to fund first?"

**Comment:**

> the harder version of this question is: which decision gets cheaper when the model is wrong?
>
> I'd fund the workflow where a bad run is visible, reversible, and cheap to retry. the interesting question isn't which project looks smartest. it's where the organization can learn fastest.

**Suggested reaction:** `INSIGHTFUL`

**Why it works:** It preserves the author's premise, reframes the question one
level deeper, and offers a test that the author can respond to.

**Do not copy:** The question must come from a real unresolved tension in the
current post, not from this AI funding scenario.

## Weak Example 1 - Generic Praise

**Weak comment:** "Great post, Alex! This is so true. AI is changing everything
and we all need to adapt. What do you think?"

**Why it fails:** It names no specific point, adds no information, uses a dead
closing prompt, and would fit almost any post.

**Rewrite:**

> Alex, the adaptation argument misses one piece.. the bottleneck is often decision rights, not awareness.
>
> when teams already know the tools matter, the next gain comes from letting one owner test a narrow workflow, measure it, and kill it without a committee. that loop is what turns interest into change.

## Weak Example 2 - Thesis Restatement

**Weak comment:** "AI isn't just a tool, it's a mindset shift. Companies that
don't embrace it will be left behind. We need to move fast and break silos."

**Why it fails:** It restates the premise, leans on a negative-parallelism
cliche, adds no evidence, and substitutes urgency for an argument.

**Rewrite:**

> you're right that adoption is slower than the demos suggest. I'd push on framing it as a mindset problem.
>
> in the teams I've watched, the blocker was usually a handoff nobody owned: one group bought the tool, another carried the risk, and no one changed the weekly review. fix the handoff and adoption starts moving.
