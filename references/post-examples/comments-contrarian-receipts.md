## Full Post

> In 1987, a structural engineer I worked with refused to let our team use finite-element software.
>
> His argument was simple. If the software got the answer, junior engineers would stop understanding the building.
>
> Three years later, he was the first person in the office asking for a license.
>
> The software did not replace judgment. It moved judgment one level up.
>
> AI coding tools are following the same path.
>
> The useful question is not whether the model can write a function. It usually can. The useful question is who can review it, explain the tradeoff, and recognize when the output is confidently wrong.
>
> That is not a junior-versus-senior story. It is a review-capacity story.
>
> Teams that hand the tool to everyone without review create speed they cannot control. Teams that ban it protect a workflow that is already disappearing.
>
> The advantage sits between those reactions. Define which decisions require human ownership. Measure the defects that reach production, not the lines generated.
>
> The engineers who treated the software as an answer source struggled. The ones who treated it as a faster way to test their judgment got stronger.
>
> What decision on your team should never be delegated to a model?
